Prepositions: at, on, in & to

English · Grammar · Theory

Prepositions like at, on, in and to often describe a similar real-world relationship — but which word you use depends on whether you're talking about place (where something is), time (when something happens), or direction (where something is moving to).

AT
  • Place: an exact point — at the bus stop, at the door, at the corner
  • Time: a precise clock time — at 5pm, at noon, at midnight
IN
  • Place: inside an enclosed space — in the room, in the box, in London
  • Time: a longer period — in July, in 2020, in summer, in the morning
ON
  • Place: touching a surface — on the table, on the wall, on the bus
  • Time: days and dates — on Monday, on 1st May, on my birthday

Direction: to, into, onto

When a verb describes movement toward a place, English usually switches to to (or into / onto for movement into or onto something) — even if the same place would use at, in or on when nothing is moving.

I go to school every morning. (movement — destination)
I am at school right now. (no movement — static point)
He walked into the room. (movement into an enclosed space)
The papers are in the room. (static — enclosed space)
The cat jumped onto the shelf. (movement onto a surface)
The cat is sleeping on the shelf. (static — on a surface)
SentencePrepositionWhy
She's going to the school.toMovement toward a destination
The lesson starts at 5pm.atA precise, exact point in time
The children are in the school.inNo movement — inside the building (enclosed space)
Watch out — common exceptions: at home, at night, at work, in bed, on time (punctual), on foot, on the phone. These don't follow the general rule above, so they need to be learned separately.
